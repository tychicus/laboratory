At the microscope / Red cell morphology

Small cells.
Different stories.

Shape, size, and color give laboratory scientists clues about red blood cells. Start with a normal film, then explore what changes—and what those changes might mean.

01 / Establish the baseline

Meet the normal field.

Most mature red cells look like round, pink discs with a lighter center, called central pallor. Their sizes are fairly similar, and they have no nucleus.

  • Outline: mostly smooth and round.
  • Center: paler because the cell is thinner here—not a hole.
  • Company: white cells and platelets share the field.

Look across a well-spread area where red cells mostly lie side by side. Thick areas and damaged edges can be misleading.

Recognize the pattern

What changed?

02 / Oval or elongated

Ovalocytes & elliptocytes

What to look for

Ovalocytes are egg-shaped; elliptocytes are longer, sometimes pencil-shaped. Look at the outline of several cells.

Possible connections

Inherited membrane-protein changes can cause hereditary elliptocytosis or ovalocytosis. Elliptocytes also occur in iron deficiency. Large oval cells (macro-ovalocytes) can accompany vitamin B12 or folate deficiency.

An oval shape is a clue. Cell size, how many cells are affected, and the rest of the smear help narrow the cause.

03 / Broken fragments

Schistocytes

What to look for

Look for sharp corners, helmet shapes, and irregular fragments instead of a complete round disc.

Possible connections

Mechanical injury can break red cells as they circulate—for example, around a mechanical heart valve or in small-vessel disorders such as TTP, HUS, or disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC).

A significant increase can be urgent. The scientist assesses fragments with the platelet count, hemolysis tests, and clinical findings—not one cell alone.

04 / A bull’s-eye pattern

Target cells

What to look for

A stained center sits inside a pale ring, with another stained rim at the edge—like a target.

Possible connections

An altered balance between cell membrane and cell contents can produce this pattern. Associations include thalassemia, hemoglobin C disease, liver disease, and absence or reduced function of the spleen.

Target cells do not identify a single disease. Hemoglobin studies, liver tests, and the patient’s history may help explain them.

05 / Echinocytes

Burr cells

What to look for

Many short, fairly uniform spikes surround the cell. This differs from the uneven projections of spur cells (acanthocytes).

Possible connections

Burr cells can form during sample storage or smear preparation. They can also occur with uremia in kidney disease and with pyruvate kinase deficiency, an inherited enzyme disorder.

Check the specimen first. A fresh, well-prepared smear helps distinguish an artifact from a finding that reflects the patient.

Connect the picture to the numbers

Anemia has more than one look.

Anemia means the hemoglobin concentration is below the appropriate reference range, reducing the blood’s oxygen-carrying capacity. It can result from blood loss, reduced production, or red cells being destroyed too quickly.

It is not a cell shape. Some anemias have striking smear changes; others have red cells that look fairly normal. The CBC helps establish anemia, and the smear helps investigate why.

06 / An example, not every anemia

When iron is limited.

Iron is needed to make hemoglobin. Established iron deficiency often produces small cells (microcytosis) with increased central pallor (hypochromia). Cell sizes and shapes may vary, and pencil-shaped cells can appear.

Possible causes include ongoing blood loss, increased iron needs, inadequate intake, or poor absorption.

The scientist connects the film with hemoglobin, MCV, RDW, and iron studies such as ferritin. Small, pale cells also occur in other conditions, including thalassemia.

Smaller / Microcytic

Think hemoglobin production.

Examples include iron deficiency and thalassemia.

Usual size / Normocytic

Normal size can still mean anemia.

Blood loss, kidney disease, and inflammation can reduce hemoglobin without an obvious size change.

Larger / Macrocytic

Look at maturation and the marrow.

Vitamin B12 or folate deficiency can produce large oval cells. Other causes of large cells include liver disease and increased reticulocytes.

What the laboratory scientist does

Put the clues together.

  1. Check the sample. Confirm identification, specimen quality, and a suitable smear area.
  2. Review the CBC. Hemoglobin assesses anemia; MCV describes average cell size; RDW reflects variation in size.
  3. Examine and report. Assess patterns across many fields and report morphology using the laboratory’s criteria.
  4. Connect and communicate. Reticulocytes, iron studies, hemoglobin testing, or hemolysis tests may clarify the pattern. Escalate significant findings according to laboratory procedure.
Discussion prompt: does an unusual cell prove disease?

No. Preparation artifacts, a few isolated abnormal cells, and overlapping disease patterns make context essential. Consider how frequent the finding is, whether it repeats in a good-quality area, and how it fits the CBC and clinical history.
