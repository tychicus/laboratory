Cell file 04 / Reactive lymphocytes

An unusual look.
A useful response.

An activated immune response

A lymphocyte can change its appearance when it responds to an immune stimulus. A reactive cell is not a leukemia cell.

Look through the lens

What do you notice?

  1. Find the central white cell

    The larger nucleated cell near the center stands out among the red cells. This image is identified by its creator as a reactive lymphocyte.

  2. Notice the cytoplasm

    Pale blue cytoplasm extends beyond the irregular nucleus. Reactive cells can vary greatly in size, shape, and staining.

  3. Look at the whole population

    A reactive response often contains a variety of appearances. One large or unusual cell does not establish a malignancy.

Stains, magnification, and sample preparation differ between photographs. Compare features, not on-screen cell sizes.

The biology

What is happening?

Normal immune activation changes how a lymphocyte works. Some activated cells enlarge and divide to help respond to a threat. DNA copying is part of that useful response; the process remains regulated.

The significance

Why does it matter?

Reactive lymphocytes can appear during infections, including infectious mononucleosis, and other immune responses. Their presence needs context. This photograph is not evidence of one specific infection.

From the microscope to the laboratory

The CBC, smear pattern, symptoms, and changes over time help frame the question. When the findings are concerning or unclear, additional studies such as flow cytometry may be needed.

Pause & think: Does an unusual-looking lymphocyte mean leukemia?

No. Activated, noncancerous lymphocytes can look striking—even blast-like. The laboratory evaluates the whole picture, rather than assigning a diagnosis from one cell or one photograph.
