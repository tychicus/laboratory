Hematology / 01

Small cells.
A vital delivery.

Red blood cells (RBCs), also called erythrocytes, carry oxygen from your lungs to your tissues and help bring carbon dioxide back for you to breathe out.

Form follows function

Built to squeeze.
Built to carry.

Its thin, indented shape gives gases a short distance to cross. Its flexible membrane lets it bend through tiny capillaries.

Inside is hemoglobin, the iron-containing protein that gives blood its red color. A mature RBC has no nucleus, leaving more space for this cargo-carrying protein.

Why it matters

Most cells use oxygen to release energy from food. RBCs keep that supply moving to your brain, muscles, and other organs.

Two gases / different routes

How does the cargo attach?

O₂ / Oxygen

Iron holds oxygen—temporarily.

Each hemoglobin molecule has four heme groups. Each heme contains an iron atom that can reversibly bind one O₂ molecule: up to four O₂ molecules per hemoglobin.

In the lungs, oxygen loads onto hemoglobin. In tissues where oxygen is lower, it is released for cells to use.

CO₂ / Carbon dioxide

Most takes a chemical detour.

Some CO₂ binds to the globin protein of hemoglobin, at different sites from oxygen—not to the heme iron. A small amount dissolves in the blood.

Most CO₂ is converted inside RBCs into bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻), helped by the enzyme carbonic anhydrase. The bicarbonate then travels mainly in plasma, the liquid part of blood.

CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻Simplified net reaction

In the lungs, this process reverses. CO₂ is re-formed, leaves the blood, and is breathed out.
