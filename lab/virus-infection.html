One genome becomes many particles.

The host cell makes viral proteins; viral enzymes copy the RNA. Assembly packages those new genomes into capsids. Lysis releases them together, giving the infection more opportunities to reach other cells.

The whole story, at your pace

  1. 01

    Attach · A compatible contact

    A viral capsid binds a receptor on a susceptible cell. Receptor matching and other cell factors determine whether infection can proceed.

  2. 02

    Enter · The cell takes the particle in

    In this simplified picornavirus-style route, the cell takes the particle into an endosome: a small compartment surrounded by membrane.

  3. 03

    Release RNA · RNA crosses into the cytoplasm

    The capsid changes shape and releases its RNA across the endosome membrane. This non-enveloped virus has no lipid envelope to fuse with the cell.

  4. 04

    Copy & build · Copy the RNA. Build the parts.

    Host ribosomes read positive-sense viral RNA to make proteins, including viral copying enzymes. Those enzymes make new genomes through a complementary RNA template. Watch RNA copies and capsid parts accumulate using the cell’s materials and energy.

  5. 05

    Assemble · Assemble many new viruses

    Capsid proteins form protective shells around new RNA genomes. Many complete particles now occupy the cytoplasm. Viruses are assembled from parts; they do not divide like bacteria.

  6. 06

    Lyse · Lysis: the cell breaks open

    In this lytic pathway, infection damages the cell and its membrane ruptures. Newly assembled viruses escape into surrounding fluid. The burst illustrates membrane damage, not a cell simply becoming too full.

  7. 07

    Spread · The infection can spread

    Released viruses can reach nearby susceptible cells, where another round of copying and assembly may begin. The two small cells illustrate possible new infections. Many particles never establish an infection; immune defenses can block or clear them.

Does every virus burst its cell?

No. This animation follows one lytic pathway. Many enveloped viruses bud from membranes, and some non-enveloped viruses can leave inside membrane-bound packages. Different viruses use different routes, and release does not always kill a cell immediately.
