Exploration 05 · Bacteria / 04

Precise treatment.
Powerful responsibility.

Antibiotics are valuable tools against bacterial infections. Using them well matters for the person today and the patients who come next.

The rule to remember

Right dose.
Right timing.
Prescribed duration.

Take antibiotics exactly as prescribed. Complete the prescribed course unless your healthcare professional tells you to change or stop it—even if you feel better sooner.

“Dose” means the amount taken at one time. “Course” means the whole planned treatment. Do not change either on your own.

How do they work?

Antibiotics can kill bacteria or stop them growing. Different drugs target different bacterial processes.

▥

Cell walls

Some drugs interfere with building the protective peptidoglycan wall.

•••

Protein production

Others act on bacterial ribosomes, disrupting the proteins cells need.

∞

DNA processes

Some disrupt DNA copying or other steps needed for bacterial growth.

Feeling better is useful—but not a dosing plan.

Symptoms alone do not tell you whether treatment is complete. Stopping or changing treatment without advice can leave an infection inadequately treated. Taking antibiotics longer than needed also carries risks. The goal is the right treatment for the right length of time, guided by the prescriber.

Resistance belongs to the bacteria.

01

Variation

A bacterial population may contain cells with resistance traits.

02

Selection

An antibiotic removes susceptible cells; resistant cells may survive.

03

Spread

Survivors can multiply. Resistance genes can also pass between bacteria.

It is the microbes—not your body—that become resistant. Unnecessary antibiotic use gives resistance more opportunities to spread.

Use them only when needed.

Antibiotics treat certain bacterial infections. They do not treat viruses such as colds or flu, and not every bacterial illness needs them.

Keep the plan personal.

Do not share antibiotics, take someone else’s, or save leftovers for a future illness. Ask your pharmacist or prescriber about missed doses or side effects.

Inside the laboratory

Culture → identify → test susceptibility.

When appropriate, the lab grows and identifies an organism, then tests which antibiotics inhibit it. These findings help the clinical team choose or adjust treatment. Your prescriber may change the drug or duration as new information arrives.

Antibiotics can also disturb helpful resident bacteria. Careful use protects more than the target infection.
