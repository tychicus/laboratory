Blood is more
than red cells.

A transfusion can replace a particular part of blood. The clinical team decides what is needed; the blood bank prepares and supplies an appropriate product.

RBC

Red cells

Carry oxygen. Used when a patient needs more oxygen-carrying capacity, such as with significant anemia or blood loss.

PLT

Platelets

Help form the first plug at an injured vessel. Used for selected patients with too few platelets or poor platelet function.

PLASMA

Plasma

The liquid portion contains clotting proteins. It can replace multiple clotting factors in appropriate bleeding situations.

CRYO

Cryoprecipitate

A concentrated source of certain clotting proteins, especially fibrinogen—the building material for a fibrin clot.

Simplified illustrations. Products have different storage conditions and expiration times; staff monitor both. Whole blood is also used in some settings under specific protocols.

The first clues

What does
“A positive” mean?

Red cells carry surface markers called antigens. In ABO typing, we look for A and B. The familiar positive or negative usually refers to another marker: RhD.

TWO SIDES OF THE SAME CHECK

Cells first. Plasma second.

Forward grouping tests the patient’s red cells with known anti-A and anti-B reagents. Reverse grouping tests the patient’s plasma against known A and B cells.

The expected patterns should agree. A discrepancy means investigation, not choosing whichever answer looks convenient. Infants and some other circumstances need different interpretation.

Agglutination is visible clumping caused by antibodies linking red cells. In a typing test, the pattern is useful evidence. An incompatible transfusion, however, can cause dangerous destruction of red cells.

Is RhD the only other blood-group antigen?

No. Many others matter, including antigens in the Kell, Kidd, and Duffy systems. Two people with the same ABO/RhD type can still be incompatible for a particular transfusion.

Change the logic.

For red cells, think about the donor’s cell antigens. For plasma, think about the donor’s antibodies. That is why their ABO compatibility patterns run in opposite directions.

Component being transfused

ABO-only teaching model. This is not a product-selection tool. RhD, other antibodies, history, special requirements, and local policy still matter. Platelets and whole blood follow additional rules.

ABO-COMPATIBLE DONOR GROUPS IN THIS MODEL
AO

For a group A recipient, group A or O red cells avoid introducing B antigen. ABO-identical components are generally preferred when available and appropriate.

Why doesn’t “universal donor” mean “any product, any patient”?

Group O red cells lack A and B antigens. AB plasma lacks the usual anti-A and anti-B antibodies. Those are different advantages for different components. Even O-negative red cells have other antigens; a compatibility chart never replaces the blood bank’s checks.

Where the scientist comes in

A blood type is
only the start.

  1. 01 / Identify

    Trust the identity.

    Check the sample, request, patient identifiers, and prior records. Resolve labeling discrepancies under policy before routine testing or issue.

  2. 02 / Screen

    Look for antibodies.

    The antibody screen looks for clinically significant non-ABO red-cell antibodies. A positive result needs further investigation.

  3. 03 / Match

    Check the unit.

    Crossmatching checks donor–recipient compatibility. A serologic method uses patient plasma and donor cells; a validated electronic method is used only when eligibility criteria are met.

  4. 04 / Issue

    Keep the chain intact.

    Verify the selected component, special requirements, and traceable issue record. The bedside team completes its own patient/product checks.

When seconds matter.

In a life-threatening emergency, authorized emergency-release protocols can supply blood before testing is complete. Scientists coordinate with the clinical team and continue the compatibility work as soon as possible.

They also manage a limited inventory, prepare components, and document every handoff. Careful communication is part of the science.

If a reaction is suspected.

The clinical team responds immediately and alerts the transfusion service. The laboratory helps investigate with identity checks, review of the unit and records, and appropriate testing. Reactions have different causes; not every reaction is an ABO mismatch.

The scientist’s work joins the bedside assessment to find out what happened and guide follow-up.
