Virology / Field notes 02

A virus isn’t
a tiny bacterium.

Bacteria are living cells. Viruses depend on living cells to reproduce. That difference changes how they grow, how we detect them, and how medicines work.

Two different kinds of microbe
FeatureVirusBacterium
Basic organizationA noncellular particle: genome, protein coat, sometimes an envelope.A single cell with a membrane, cytoplasm, DNA, and ribosomes.
Making proteinsUses a host cell’s ribosomes.Has its own ribosomes.
Making moreCopies its genome and assembles new particles inside a host cell.Usually grows and divides into two cells by binary fission.
Genetic materialA genome made of DNA or RNA.A DNA genome; RNA helps the cell use those instructions.
Independent activityNo independent metabolism or reproduction.Has metabolism. Many grow outside other cells; some require a host.
Seeing themMost are too small to resolve with a standard light microscope.Many can be seen with a light microscope and suitable staining.
AntibioticsAntibacterial antibiotics do not work on viruses.Some bacterial infections respond to antibiotics; resistance and the organism matter.

Different biology. Different targets.

Antibacterial antibiotics act on bacterial structures or processes. Viruses lack those targets. Antiviral medicines, when available, interfere with specific steps of a viral infection. Vaccines train immune defenses before a future encounter.

“Microbe” doesn’t mean “enemy.”

Many bacteria help us digest food or occupy space that harmful microbes might otherwise use. A virus can infect humans, animals, plants, or microbes; bacteriophages infect bacteria.

Same symptom, different cause.

A cough or fever does not identify the organism. Laboratory tests can look for genetic material, proteins, or other evidence of infection.
