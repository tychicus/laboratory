What if bacteria
were car-sized?

Same doubling clock. Much bigger cells. Watch an everyday-sized beginning become an astronomical volume.

The rules of this thought experiment

Start with one bacterium. Double every 20 minutes for 24 hours. Give every cell a car-sized volume: 5 m × 2 m × 1.5 m = 15 m³. Assume unlimited resources.

The cube represents volume, not the shape of one car. View automatically zooms out as the population grows.

Animation compresses 24 hours into about 22 seconds. You can also drag the time slider.

At the 24-hour finish line

4.72 sextillion cells.
A cube wider than three Earths.

72 doublings → 4,722,366,482,869,645,213,696 cells.

At 15 m³ each, their combined volume fills a cube about 25,700 miles on each side. That edge is about 3.24 times Earth’s equatorial diameter (about 7,926 miles).

A volume comparison—not a possible parking lot.

We use a tightly packed cube with no gaps to make “how big is the pile?” measurable. A loose heap would take up more space. The model ignores gravity, crushing, and the biological impossibility of car-sized bacteria. The repeating marks on the cube are texture, not individual counted cells.

Show the calculation

24 × 60 ÷ 20 = 72 generations.
Number of cells = 272.
Combined volume = 15 × 272 m³.
Cube edge in miles = ∛(15 × 272) ÷ 1,609.344 ≈ 25,710 miles.
Conversion: 1 mile = 1,609.344 meters.
