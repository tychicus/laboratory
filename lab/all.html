Cell file 03 / ALL

A different lineage.
Another interrupted future.

Acute lymphoblastic leukemia

In ALL, abnormal immature lymphoid cells—lymphoblasts—accumulate. Testing determines whether they belong to B- or T-cell lineage.

Look through the lens

What do you notice?

  1. Nuclei dominate the cells

    Find the cluster of purple cells toward the lower half of the photo. Their nuclei occupy much of each cell.

  2. A narrow blue rim

    Only a limited amount of cytoplasm surrounds several nuclei. Blast appearance can vary considerably.

  3. A resemblance that needs testing

    ALL and AML blasts can overlap in appearance. A microscope image alone cannot reliably identify every blast’s lineage.

Stains, magnification, and sample preparation differ between photographs. Compare features, not on-screen cell sizes.

The biology

What is happening?

Genetic changes can disturb the development and growth of lymphoid precursor cells. These immature cells accumulate rather than developing into normally functioning lymphocytes.

The significance

Why does it matter?

Leukemia can disrupt the marrow’s supply of normal red cells, platelets, and white cells. ALL is an important childhood leukemia, but it also occurs in adults.

From the microscope to the laboratory

Flow cytometry uses patterns of B-cell, T-cell, and immaturity markers to help classify the blasts. Genetic testing further characterizes the disease. This slide’s source does not specify B-ALL or T-ALL, so the photograph is labeled simply ALL.

Pause & think: Can you identify B-ALL versus T-ALL just by this photo?

No. Both can produce similar-looking lymphoblasts. Immunophenotyping, usually by flow cytometry, helps establish lineage. That is why the laboratory combines appearance with protein markers and genetic findings.
