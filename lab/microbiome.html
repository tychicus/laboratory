Exploration 05 · Bacteria / 05

Our smallest
everyday partners.

Most bacteria are not the villains of the story. Many are part of the communities we live with every day.

Normal flora → resident microbiota

You are an ecosystem.

“Normal flora” is an older name for the microbes that usually live in and on us. “Microbiota” describes the organisms; “microbiome” often includes their genes and environment. These communities include bacteria, along with fungi, viruses, and other microbes.

01 / SKIN

Life at the surface

Resident microbes occupy space and use resources that potential invaders also need. Conditions differ between dry skin, oily areas, and moist folds.

02 / MOUTH

A busy neighborhood

Teeth, tongue, and saliva support different communities. Balance matters: some bacteria can contribute to tooth decay and gum disease.

03 / GUT

A metabolic partnership

Dense communities in the large intestine process food leftovers and interact with the gut lining and immune system.

The GI tract

What happens to the leftovers?

YOU DIGEST & ABSORB

Small intestine

Your enzymes break down food, and the small intestine absorbs most nutrients. Our microbes do not do all the digestion for us.

↓
MICROBES PROCESS WHAT REMAINS

Large intestine

Bacteria ferment some carbohydrates we cannot fully digest, including certain fibers. Fermentation produces short-chain fatty acids and gases.

↓
THE PARTNERSHIP PAYS OFF

Fuel, signals & nutrients

Some fermentation products fuel cells lining the colon. Gut microbes also make certain vitamins, including vitamin K, and help shape immune activity.

Occupy the space

Established communities can help limit the growth of incoming disease-causing organisms.

Support the barrier

Microbial products interact with the gut lining, helping maintain a working boundary between the gut contents and the body.

Train the response

Microbes help the immune system develop and respond to its surroundings.

Helpful here can be harmful there.

A normal resident is not automatically harmless everywhere. Bacteria that belong in the gut may cause infection if they enter the urinary tract, bloodstream, or another normally protected site. Location and the person’s health matter.

There is no single “perfect” microbiome. Communities vary with diet, age, medicines, and environment.
