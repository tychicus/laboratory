Cell file 01 / AML

Too many cells.
Too few ready for work.

Acute myeloid leukemia

A blast is an immature precursor cell. In AML, abnormal cells of the myeloid lineage accumulate and interfere with normal blood production.

Look through the lens

What do you notice?

  1. A large nucleus

    Many cells have a nucleus that occupies much of the cell. The surrounding blue area is cytoplasm.

  2. Immature nuclear detail

    Some nuclei have fine-looking chromatin and pale nucleoli—the small structures inside the nucleus.

  3. A clue called an Auer rod

    The source identifies these slender inclusions in some cells. Their presence supports myeloid differentiation; their absence does not rule out AML.

Stains, magnification, and sample preparation differ between photographs. Compare features, not on-screen cell sizes.

The biology

What is happening?

DNA changes can disrupt the programs that control cell development and growth. Immature cells accumulate instead of producing an effective, balanced supply of mature blood cells.

The significance

Why does it matter?

Normal marrow production may fall. Low red cells can mean anemia; low platelets can mean bleeding; too few effective infection-fighting cells can mean infections. A high WBC count does not guarantee strong immune defenses.

From the microscope to the laboratory

Flow cytometry examines patterns of proteins on and within cells. Chromosome and molecular tests add information that helps classify AML. The smear supplies clues; the diagnosis uses the combined findings.

Pause & think: Are all blasts cancer cells?

No. Normal bone marrow contains a small population of immature precursors. Their number, location, appearance, protein patterns, and genetic findings determine whether an abnormal process is present. A blast is a stage of development, not automatically a diagnosis.
